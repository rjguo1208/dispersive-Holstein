A simple model of an isolated defect
Local electronic states, phonon states and electron–phonon coupling

Place one substitutional defect in a Holstein-type lattice and ask how it changes the local electronic states, the local phonon states and the electron–phonon coupling.

Theory design notes, 2026-09-23. This page discusses only the model and the reasoning; nothing has been implemented or numerically validated yet. Defect excited states are not considered, so the Huang–Rhys model is not used; only the ground state and static response are examined. Long equations and figures scroll horizontally.

1. Conclusion

A single electron, a tight-binding band and dispersive optical phonons (one spring from each site to the ground and one spring between neighbouring sites), with a local linear electron–phonon coupling; place one substitutional defect on site 0.

The defect is described by four microscopic parameters: the on-site potential εd\varepsilon_dεd​, the coupling αd\alpha_dαd​, the stiffness to ground KdK_dKd​ and the mass MdM_dMd​.

Compare the ground state and static response in only two cases: N=0N=0N=0 (no electron on the defect) and N=1N=1N=1 (the electron is trapped by the defect). Defect excited states are then never needed.

3. Why these ingredients

3.1 The phonons need dispersion

In the Einstein model the oscillator at the defect is not coupled to any other phonon. A "local phonon state" then just means one frequency changed by hand: there is no distinction between a local mode and a continuum, no localization length, no resonance, and the phonon local density of states at the defect is always a δ\deltaδ function.

Only after adding the neighbour springs KKK do local vibrational modes, gap modes and resonant modes appear. [2] Dispersion also removes a degeneracy of Einstein phonons: the states "trapped electron plus one phonon on any distant site" all have exactly the same energy in the Einstein model.

K0>0K_0\gt0K0​>0 opens a gap at the bottom of the phonon band, and this is equally necessary. It guarantees that a softer or heavier defect produces a genuinely localized gap mode, the kind of mode most relevant to trapping. In an acoustic chain (K0=0K_0=0K0​=0) such defects give only in-band resonances.

3.2 Parametrize with (α,K,M)(\alpha,K,M)(α,K,M), not (g,ω)(g,\omega)(g,ω)

ωd=Kd/Md,gd=αd2Md ωd,Ep,d=gd2ωd=αd22Kd.\omega_d=\sqrt{K_d/M_d},\qquad g_d=\frac{\alpha_d}{\sqrt{2M_d\,\omega_d}},\qquad E_{p,d}=\frac{g_d^2}{\omega_d}=\frac{\alpha_d^2}{2K_d}.ωd​=Kd​/Md​​,gd​=2Md​ωd​​αd​​,Ep,d​=ωd​gd2​​=2Kd​αd2​​.

The last expression does not depend on the mass. A common practice is to assign (ωd,gd)(\omega_d,g_d)(ωd​,gd​) to the defect site and use the strong-coupling (Lang–Firsov) estimate [3]

εeff=−(gd2ωd−g2ω0).\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}=-\Big(\frac{g_d^2}{\omega_d}-\frac{g^2}{\omega_0}\Big).εeff​=−(ωd​gd2​​−ω0​g2​).

But "lowering ωd\omega_dωd​ at fixed gdg_dgd​" changes both the coupling and the stiffness at the microscopic level. The statement "phonon softening traps the electron" has to be split:

  • A softer stiffness (at fixed α\alphaα and MMM) deepens the trap.
  • The "softening" from a heavier mass (an isotope) does not deepen the trap statically; it enters only through non-adiabatic corrections.

The phonon side therefore needs two independent parameters: the stiffness controls static effects and the mass controls dynamical ones.

3.3 A single electron: compare the ground states for N=0N=0N=0 and N=1N=1N=1

  • N=0N=0N=0 is a pure-phonon defect problem and gives the phonon states of the defect itself.
  • N=1N=1N=1 is the electron trapped by the defect, that is, the trapped polaron.
  • Their difference is the change "caused by the electron".

The Huang–Rhys model deals with the difference between the equilibrium configurations of two electronic states. Here there is only the ground state of one electronic configuration, so it is not needed.

3.4 The host coupling can be switched off separately

With α=0\alpha=0α=0 and αd≠0\alpha_d\neq0αd​=0, the model is exactly a spinless Anderson–Holstein impurity model with a phonon bath [4][5]: the electronic orbital at the defect hybridizes with a tight-binding bath, the oscillator at the defect couples to a phonon bath through springs, and the two are connected only by the local coupling αdn0u0\alpha_d n_0u_0αd​n0​u0​. This is the minimal core of all three changes.

Once α\alphaα is switched on, the defect sits in a host that has its own electron–phonon coupling, and the electron is a polaron even away from the defect.

4.2 Curvature of the Born–Oppenheimer surface

Φeff=Φ−Dα χ Dα,Dα=diag(αj),χjl=−∂ρj∂εl.\Phi_{\mathrm{eff}}=\Phi-D_\alpha\,\chi\,D_\alpha,\qquad D_\alpha=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_j),\qquad \chi_{jl}=-\frac{\partial\rho_j}{\partial\varepsilon_l}.Φeff​=Φ−Dα​χDα​,Dα​=diag(αj​),χjl​=−∂εl​∂ρj​​.

χ\chiχ is the static density response of the electronic ground state and is positive semidefinite. Φeff\Phi_{\mathrm{eff}}Φeff​ describes how the trapped electron softens the local stiffness; this is purely a ground-state property.

With linear coupling, an electron fully localized on one site only shifts the oscillator and does not change its frequency (a displaced oscillator). An electron-induced change of frequency can therefore come only from the polarizability χ\chiχ, i.e. from the hopping ttt. Without electron–phonon coupling, the one-dimensional Koster–Slater bound state [6] has ⟨n0⟩=∣εd∣/4t2+εd2\langle n_0\rangle=|\varepsilon_d|/\sqrt{4t^2+\varepsilon_d^2}⟨n0​⟩=∣εd​∣/4t2+εd2​​, hence

For deep traps χ00\chi_{00}χ00​ decays quickly, and the electron-induced softening disappears with it.

5. A quantitative definition of the change in coupling

For the bound state BBB, define its coupling to each phonon eigenmode λ\lambdaλ (frequency ωλ\omega_\lambdaωλ​, mass-weighted eigenvector eλ(j)e_\lambda(j)eλ​(j)) and the coupling spectral function:

When the electron sits entirely on the defect site, this reduces to

J0(ω)=αd22Md ω ρ00ph(ω),J_0(\omega)=\frac{\alpha_d^2}{2M_d\,\omega}\,\rho^{\mathrm{ph}}_{00}(\omega),J0​(ω)=2Md​ωαd2​​ρ00ph​(ω),

where ρ00ph\rho^{\mathrm{ph}}_{00}ρ00ph​ is the phonon local density of states at the defect (mass-weighted, integrating to 1). In other words, the coupling spectrum at the defect is "the change in the coupling constant" times "the change in the phonon local density of states at the defect". The relaxation energy ErelE_{\mathrm{rel}}Erel​ contains only Φ−1\Phi^{-1}Φ−1 and is likewise independent of the mass.

Splitting JBJ_BJB​ into the δ\deltaδ peak of the local mode and the band continuum, the fraction flocf_{\mathrm{loc}}floc​ of the relaxation energy carried by the local mode is controlled by ξe/ξph\xi_e/\xi_{\mathrm{ph}}ξe​/ξph​. This fraction answers quantitatively whether the coupling at the defect is dominated by the local mode.

JBJ_BJB​ is also the input for the Huang–Rhys factor. Here it is used only for ground-state questions: how large the relaxation energy is, and which modes dress the bound state.

6. Control parameters and physical regimes

The adiabaticity of the defect is set by ωloc/Ebind\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}/E_{\mathrm{bind}}ωloc​/Ebind​, not by the host ratio ω0/t\omega_0/tω0​/t. EbindE_{\mathrm{bind}}Ebind​ is the distance from the defect level to the band edge, i.e. the minimum energy needed to excite the electron from the defect into the band.

  • Shallow level (Ebind≪ωlocE_{\mathrm{bind}}\ll\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}Ebind​≪ωloc​, ξe≫ξph\xi_e\gg\xi_{\mathrm{ph}}ξe​≫ξph​): the electron sees an averaged lattice, the effective coupling is weak, and most of the dressing comes from band phonons. For a weak defect in one dimension the leading-order binding energy is Ebind≃m∗εd2/2E_{\mathrm{bind}}\simeq m^*\varepsilon_d^2/2Ebind​≃m∗εd2​/2, with m∗m^*m∗ the polaron effective mass.
  • Deep level (Ebind≫ωlocE_{\mathrm{bind}}\gg\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}Ebind​≫ωloc​): the Born–Oppenheimer approximation holds, static relaxation dominates, and the softening (∝α2χ00\propto\alpha^2\chi_{00}∝α2χ00​) is small, close to a displaced oscillator. This is where the Huang–Rhys model naturally applies; this page stays with the ground state.
  • Intermediate regime (Ebind∼ωlocE_{\mathrm{bind}}\sim\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}Ebind​∼ωloc​, ξe∼ξph∼1\xi_e\sim\xi_{\mathrm{ph}}\sim1ξe​∼ξph​∼1): the electron and the local mode form a "molecule". The electron-induced softening and the isotope effect are expected to be largest here: inside the Born–Oppenheimer regime the softening decreases as the trap deepens, while for shallower traps the Born–Oppenheimer approximation itself fails. The three changes are most strongly entangled here, which makes this regime the most worth studying.

6.1 Cooperative feedback and thresholds

The more localized the electron, the stronger the coupling; the stronger the coupling, the deeper the trap; the deeper the trap, the more localized the electron. Whether this feedback produces a threshold depends on the dimension and on the type of defect.

An example that can be worked out directly: take α=0\alpha=0α=0, εd=0\varepsilon_d=0εd​=0 and the Einstein limit, so that there is coupling only at the defect. The adiabatic functional becomes

Ead[ψ]=⟨ψ∣Hkin∣ψ⟩−Ep,d ρ02,E_{\mathrm{ad}}[\psi]=\langle\psi|H_{\mathrm{kin}}|\psi\rangle-E_{p,d}\,\rho_0^2 ,Ead​[ψ]=⟨ψ∣Hkin​∣ψ⟩−Ep,d​ρ02​,

The effective on-site potential is −2Ep,d ρ0-2E_{p,d}\,\rho_0−2Ep,d​ρ0​. Inserting the one-dimensional Koster–Slater result ρ0=∣ε∣/4t2+ε2\rho_0=|\varepsilon|/\sqrt{4t^2+\varepsilon^2}ρ0​=∣ε∣/4t2+ε2​ and solving self-consistently gives 4t2+ε2=2Ep,d\sqrt{4t^2+\varepsilon^2}=2E_{p,d}4t2+ε2​=2Ep,d​. Hence for a classical lattice there is a threshold Ep,d=tE_{p,d}=tEp,d​=t even in one dimension; below the threshold the electron is not trapped. For the self-trapping transition of a single nonlinear impurity, see also [9].

With quantum phonons this threshold disappears. The second-order self-energy

Σ00(E)=gd2 G000(E−ωd)\Sigma_{00}(E)=g_d^2\,G^{0}_{00}(E-\omega_d)Σ00​(E)=gd2​G000​(E−ωd​)

is a finite local attraction near the band bottom, and in one dimension any attraction binds the electron. Taking ωd→0\omega_d\to0ωd​→0 at fixed Ep,dE_{p,d}Ep,d​ makes this attraction vanish, and the threshold reappears. The difference between the two conclusions is precisely a non-adiabatic effect.

In two and three dimensions, or on a Bethe lattice, self-trapping in the host itself already has thresholds and bistability, and a defect or a cluster of defects can trigger self-trapping. [8] Calculations of three-dimensional Holstein polarons trapped by various types of impurities are given in [7]. The one-dimensional model has no such threshold (apart from the classical-lattice example above), which makes it a controlled benchmark.

8. Order of control experiments

  1. Start with α=αd=0\alpha=\alpha_d=0α=αd​=0. The electronic side is the Koster–Slater problem and the phonon side is a local-vibrational-mode problem; the two T-matrices are independent and both are solvable analytically. [6][2] First map ξe\xi_eξe​, ξph\xi_{\mathrm{ph}}ξph​ and ωloc\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}ωloc​ as functions of each parameter.
  2. Switch on the coupling one parameter at a time, and check the table in Section 4 cell by cell.
  3. Compare the adiabatic limit with the quantum lattice. Take M→∞M\to\inftyM→∞ at fixed stiffness and compare with the quantum result: whatever does not depend on the mass is static physics, the rest is dynamical.
  4. Focus on the intermediate regime: Ebind∼ωlocE_{\mathrm{bind}}\sim\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}Ebind​∼ωloc​, ξe∼ξph\xi_e\sim\xi_{\mathrm{ph}}ξe​∼ξph​.

When implementing, note that MdM_dMd​, KdK_dKd​ and αd\alpha_dαd​ act on the phonons at the defect, so the embedding equations no longer close on the subspace "electron on the defect site"; they need the subspace "phonons at the defect, electron anywhere". For these parameters, direct diagonalization or a variational calculation centred on the defect is therefore more natural than a T-matrix embedding.

9. What is left out for now

  • Several defect orbitals. They bring in excited states, Jahn–Teller and Huang–Rhys physics. The present ground bound state is even about site 0 and couples linearly only to even modes; odd modes participate only once bond-type coupling or degenerate orbitals are introduced.
  • Bond-type (SSH) coupling. Needed if what the defect changes is a "bond".
  • Quadratic coupling γ n0u02\gamma\,n_0u_0^2γn0​u02​. Needed to make "occupation changes the stiffness" an independent parameter.
  • Spin and UUU. The next step if the interest later shifts to the charge states of the defect rather than its excited states. The classic ground-state effect is a negative UUU: in the local limit Ueff=U−2Ep,dU_{\mathrm{eff}}=U-2E_{p,d}Ueff​=U−2Ep,d​. [10]
  • Finite temperature, finite electron density and anharmonic effects.
